# Q6 — Graduate addendum

**Graduate teams answer this. Undergraduate teams do not.** It is graded, and it is the
part that is hardest to fake, because it asks us to say in advance what would prove us
wrong.

The brief asks for four things:

> A falsifiable definition is a definition that evidence can prove wrong.
>
> 1. A falsifiable definition of need, stated plainly.
> 2. An explanation of why your new metric captures need rather than demand.
> 3. The evidence that would make you reject the definition.
> 4. The evidence that would make you revise the measure.

---

## Why this is worth real time

Any team can build an index. Almost no team will state, before seeing the result, what
would make them abandon it. This section is where a graduate submission separates itself,
and it is also the section a judge is most likely to push on live — because it is the one
where a weak answer is obvious.

Write it **before** the index is finished. A falsifiability criterion invented after you
know the answer is not one.

---

## 1. A falsifiable definition of need

Fill this in. It has to be a sentence that could turn out to be false.

> **Our definition:** _______________________________________________

Tests for whether your definition is actually falsifiable:

- Does it refer to a **state of households**, not to a number in our dataset? ("Need is
  what our index measures" is circular.)
- Could a survey, in principle, **measure it directly** and disagree with us?
- Does it distinguish need from demand **in a way you could observe**?

A starting shape, not an answer: *a county's need is the number of households that would
use a food shelf if distance, information and stigma were not barriers.* That is
falsifiable — remove a barrier in one county and demand should move toward our estimate.
Improve on it.

## 2. Why the metric captures need rather than demand

Argue this **input by input**. For each component of the index, answer:

- Is this measurable **in a county where nobody visits a food shelf**? (If not, it is
  contaminated by demand.)
- Is it already inside the Map the Meal Gap model? (If so, it adds nothing.)
- What mechanism connects it to unmet need, in one sentence?

**Wilkin County is the test case.** It has no reporting food shelf at all. Our measure
must produce a sensible need estimate for Wilkin. If it produces zero, or nothing, the
measure is a demand measure with extra steps.

## 3. Evidence that would make us reject the definition

State this concretely and in advance. Examples of the right shape:

- If a household survey in our high-divergence counties found food-insecurity rates **at
  or below** the state average, the definition is wrong.
- If counties we rank as high-need showed **no increase in demand** after a new site
  opened within reach, the definition is wrong — the unmet need we posited was not there.
- If our divergence turned out to be fully explained by **cross-county travel** (residents
  using a neighbouring county's shelf), then we measured a reporting artefact, not need.

## 4. Evidence that would make us revise the measure

Weaker than rejection: the definition survives, the implementation does not.

- The county ranking is **unstable** under reasonable alternative weights.
- One input **dominates** the index — it is that input wearing a costume.
- A component turns out to be **partly downstream of visits** (contamination).
- Better data arrives at a **finer grain**, making a county-level index the wrong object.

## How this gets presented

One slide. The definition in a sentence, the rejection criterion in a sentence, and an
honest statement of which parts of the measure we are least confident in. Volunteering
the weakness is how you stop a judge from finding it.

Everyone on the team must be able to state the definition and the rejection criterion
without notes. Judges may direct this question at any of us.
